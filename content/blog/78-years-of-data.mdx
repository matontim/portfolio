# “Three generations, 78 years of data”

[The Framingham Heart Study](https://www.framinghamheartstudy.org/2026/07/01/research-on-tap-the-framingham-heart-study-three-generations-78-years-of-data-and-more-to-discover/), launched by the National Institute of Health (NIH) in 1948, produced the concept of cardiovascular risk factors as we know them today, reshaping how we treat and prevent heart disease.
That kind of science requires something specific from its funder: patience, and a stable set of rules about how funding decisions get made and changed.
That stability is currently the subject of a live federal policy debate, and it's a good lens for viewing how grant funding actually works, independent of who's in office.
In May 2026, the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) [proposed changes](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/29/2026-10817/regulation-for-federal-financial-assistance) to how federal agencies award and administer research grants. Boston University submitted [formal public comments](https://www.bu.edu/federal/files/2026/07/BU.OMB_.07.10.26.pdf) opposing several provisions. As of the July 13 close of the comment period, OMB had received over 292,000 public comments. 
Three changes are at the center of the debate:

<strong>Post-award political review:</strong> The rule would allow designated senior appointees to terminate a grant after it's been awarded and research is underway, without being required to give detailed reasons, and with limited avenues for appeal.

<strong>A new layer of pre-issuance review:</strong> Agencies would have to designate senior appointees to review discretionary awards before they're issued, checking that they demonstrably advance the President's priorities. Critics note the criteria for this review are rather subjective and aren't clearly defined.

<strong>Reduced support for international collaboration:</strong> The rule would curb federal funding for certain research activities involving scientists abroad.

## What matters scientifically?

OMB's stated rationale is straightforward: increase transparency and accountability, and reduce regulatory burden in how grants are administered. That's a legitimate policy goal, and it's worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. Bureaucratic friction in federal funding is a real, well-documented problem that researchers across the political spectrum complain about.
As a matter of how science operates, three mechanical concerns are worth understanding: 

### Peer review is the determinant for “good” science
Federal grants are largely awarded and renewed based on review by scientists in the field, not by political staff. The proposed rule doesn’t eliminate peer review, but rather adds a layer of appointee review on top of it. Whether that layer will change outcomes is an open question, but what it clearly changes is who has to sign off. 

### Grants are multi-year commitments
A 4-year R01 grant isn't one decision; it's a decision plus 3-4 renewal checkpoints. If continuation funding can be paused or terminated without detailed justification, the “4-year grant” is actually something closer to a series of shorter, less certain commitments. That matters most for long-term projects, like the Framingham Study, that only pay off after a decade or more.

### BU’s letter points to a concrete precedent, not a hypothetical
According to BU’s letter, unexpected grant terminations last year already forced some BU labs to cut personnel, halt data collection, and abandon projects midstream. The letter argues that this undermined the federal government’s own initial investment in that work. Additionally, science is increasingly collaborative across borders. One example cited in the university’s letter is BU physicists working with CERN’s Large Hadron Collider. Restricting funding for projects with international collaboration changes who can afford to participate in them, and on what terms.

## Broader public impacts

Most people have never read a grant proposal, so it's easy to assume that this is an inside fight about paperwork, however this is far from the truth. Grant funding decides which diseases get studied, which treatments reach a doctor’s office, and which discoveries get published for others to build upon. 

### Disease and public health. 
Framingham didn’t just describe heart disease: it's the reason your doctor checks your blood pressure and cholesterol at all, and the reason “risk factor” is a phrase in medical vocabulary. If continuation for that kind of study can be cut mid-stream with limited appeal, the studies most likely to survive are those with fast and visible results, not necessarily the ones most likely to change medicine 20 years from now. The diseases that take the longest to understand are often the ones that this kind of instability and uncertainty hits hardest. 

### Access to research
In its [letter to OMB](https://www.bu.edu/federal/files/2026/07/BU.OMB_.07.10.26.pdf), BU’s specifically warns that this proposed rule will create “barriers to the public dissemination of federally funded research.” Federal grants typically come with the expectation that the findings become publicly available, considering taxpayers fund the work. Research that’s interrupted doesn't get published, and the public never sees it.

### The economy and the workforce
Federally funded labs are also training grounds for the next generation of scientists. Grant terminations mean lost jobs for research staff or abandoned thesis projects for graduate students. Universities and biotech companies draw on federally funded basic research as the raw material for their products, and an unpredictable pipeline shows up in drug development timelines and medical devices. 

### International standing 
Restricting funding tied to international collaboration not only affects the specific projects involved (like BU’s work with CERN), but it also affects whether the U.S. remains an attractive partner for large-scale research. Collaborators who can't count on stable participation may build those partnerships elsewhere.  

## The current standing

The public comment period closed July 13, 2026. OMB will now review 292,000+ comments before finalizing, revising, or withdrawing their proposed rule. As [BU Today reported](https://www.bu.edu/articles/2026/bu-leaders-letter-federal-government-grant-approval-process/), the outcome isn't decided yet. As the process unfolds, viewers can watch in real time how a federal agency, universities, scientific societies, and other stakeholders each make their case for how discretionary funding decisions should be governed.
